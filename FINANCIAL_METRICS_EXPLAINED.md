## 1. Stock Price and Market Cap

Before understanding metrics, you need two foundational concepts:

**Stock Price** = The current trading price per share (e.g., $150 per share of Apple)

**Market Cap (Market Capitalization)** = Total value of the company

```
Market Cap = Stock Price × Number of Shares Outstanding
```

**Example:**
- Apple stock price: $190
- Shares outstanding: ~15.5 billion
- Market Cap = $190 × 15,500,000,000 = $2.945 trillion

**Why Market Cap Matters:**
| Size | Example | Risk Level |
|------|---------|------------|
| Mega Cap | > $200B (Apple, Microsoft) | Lower risk, less growth |
| Large Cap | $10B - $200B | Moderate risk |
| Mid Cap | $2B - $10B | Higher risk, more growth |
| Small Cap | < $2B | Highest risk, most volatile |
| Micro Cap | < $300M | Speculative |

## 2. Earnings Basics

### Net Income

**What it is:** The company's profit after ALL expenses, taxes, and costs are deducted from revenue.

**Think of it as:** Your take-home pay after taxes, rent, food, insurance, etc.

```
Net Income = Revenue - All Costs (COGS + Operating Expenses + Interest + Taxes)
```

**Example - Apple FY2024 (simplified):**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Revenue | $391 billion |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $213 billion |
| Operating Expenses | $58 billion |
| Interest & Taxes | $15 billion |
| **Net Income** | **$105 billion** |

**What to look for:**
- Consistent growth over 3-5 years
- Net income should generally follow revenue growth (or exceed it via margin improvement)
- Be wary of net income growth much faster than revenue (possible accounting tricks)

---

### EPS (Earnings Per Share)

**What it is:** How much profit the company makes per share of stock.

**Why it matters:** A $10 billion company with 1 billion shares has $10 EPS. A $10 billion company with 100 million shares has $100 EPS. EPS normalizes for company size.

```
EPS = Net Income / Shares Outstanding
```

**Example:**
| Company | Market Cap | Net Income | Shares Outstanding | EPS |
|---------|-----------|------------|-------------------|-----|
| Apple | $2.9T | $105B | 15.5B | $6.77 |
| Microsoft | $3.1T | $88B | 7.43B | $11.84 |
| Amazon | $2.0T | $30B | 10.3B | $2.90 |

**Trailing EPS vs Forward EPS:**
- **Trailing EPS** = Actual EPS from the past 12 months
- **Forward EPS** = Analyst/management forecast for next 12 months

## 3. Valuation Multiples

Valuation multiples answer: "Is this stock cheap or expensive compared to..."

### P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings)

**What it is:** How much investors pay for $1 of earnings. The most common valuation metric.

```
P/E = Stock Price / EPS
```

**Example:**
| Company | Stock Price | EPS (TTM) | P/E Ratio |
|---------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| Apple | $190 | $6.77 | 28.1x |
| Microsoft | $415 | $11.84 | 35.0x |
| NVIDIA | $130 | $2.45 | 53.1x |
| Bank of America | $40 | $3.11 | 12.9x |

**How to interpret P/E:**

| P/E Range | Interpretation | Example |
|-----------|---------------|---------|
| < 10x | Cheap (value) or troubled | Bank of America at 12.9x |
| 10-20x | Fair value | Typical for mature companies |
| 20-30x | Growth premium | Apple at 28x |
| 30-50x | Expensive growth | NVIDIA at 53x |
| > 50x | Very expensive / speculative | High-growth speculative |

**Key insight:** A LOW P/E doesn't automatically mean "buy." It might mean the market thinks earnings will fall. Always ask: "Why is the P/E low?"

**Pitfalls:**
- P/E can be low because earnings are expected to collapse
- Different sectors have naturally different P/E ranges (tech > banks > utilities)
- Always compare P/E within the same sector

---

### PEG Ratio

**What it is:** P/E ratio adjusted for growth rate. Answers: "Is this stock cheap given its growth rate?"

```
PEG = P/E / Annual EPS Growth Rate (%)
```

**Example:**
| Company | P/E | EPS Growth | PEG | Interpretation |
|---------|-----|-----------|-----|----------------|
| Apple | 28x | 10% | 2.8 | Expensive for growth |
| NVIDIA | 53x | 50% | 1.1 | Fair value for growth |
| Amazon | 65x | 25% | 2.6 | Expensive for growth |

**How to interpret PEG:**

| PEG | Interpretation |
|-----|---------------|
| < 1.0 | Potentially undervalued given growth |
| 1.0 - 1.5 | Fairly valued |
| 1.5 - 2.0 | Slightly expensive |
| > 2.0 | Expensive; growth priced in |

**Pitfall:** PEG assumes growth is sustainable. A company growing 50% but in a declining industry might not deserve the same PEG as a company growing 50% in an expanding industry.

---

### P/B Ratio (Price-to-Book)

**What it is:** Price relative to book value (what the company would be worth if liquidated).

```
P/B = Stock Price / Book Value Per Share
```

**Book Value Per Share = (Total Assets - Total Liabilities) / Shares Outstanding**

**Example:**
| Company | Stock Price | Book Value/Share | P/B |
|---------|-------------|------------------|-----|
| Apple | $190 | $4.25 | 44.7x |
| Bank of America | $40 | $33.50 | 1.2x |
| Intel | $30 | $24.00 | 1.25x |

**Why is Apple's P/B so high?** Apple has very little book value (its value is brand, IP, future earnings) compared to its market cap. Banks have lots of assets (loans, investments) on their books, so P/B near 1x means you're paying roughly book value.

**How to interpret P/B:**

| P/B | Interpretation |
|-----|---------------|
| < 1x | Trading below book value (potential value play) |
| 1x - 3x | Normal range for most companies |
| 3x - 5x | Premium for intangible-heavy businesses |
| > 5x | Very high premium |

**Pitfalls:**
- Book value can be inflated by assets that are worth less than accounting value (goodwill impairment)
- Tech companies with minimal assets always have high P/B — that's normal
- Useful primarily for financial companies, utilities, and asset-heavy businesses

---

### P/S Ratio (Price-to-Sales)

**What it is:** Price relative to annual revenue. Useful when a company has no earnings yet.

```
P/S = Market Cap / Annual Revenue
```

**Example:**
| Company | Market Cap | Revenue | P/S |
|---------|-----------|---------|-----|
| Apple | $2.9T | $391B | 7.4x |
| Amazon | $2.0T | $590B | 3.4x |
| Tesla | $780B | $90B | 8.7x |
| NVIDIA | $3.2T | $60B | 53x |

**When to use P/S:**
- Companies with no earnings (startups, turnaround situations)
- Comparing companies in same industry with different profitability
- High P/S for profitable companies is a red flag

**Pitfalls:**
- Sales without profit are vanity; low P/S doesn't mean cheap if margins are terrible
- Better for comparing similar businesses than as a standalone metric

---

### EV/EBITDA

**What it is:** Enterprise Value divided by EBITDA. More complete than P/E because it accounts for debt and cash.

**First, let's understand Enterprise Value (EV):**

```
EV = Market Cap + Total Debt - Cash
```

**Think of it as:** What it would cost to buy the whole business (including taking on its debt while getting its cash).

**Example - Tesla:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Market Cap | $780 billion |
| Plus: Total Debt | $15 billion |
| Minus: Cash | $30 billion |
| **EV** | **$765 billion** |

**Now EBITDA:**

```
EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization
```

Think of it as: Operating cash profit before the costs of capital investments and taxes.

**Example - Tesla EV/EBITDA:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| EV | $765 billion |
| EBITDA (TTM) | $15 billion |
| **EV/EBITDA** | **51x** |

**How to interpret EV/EBITDA:**

| EV/EBITDA | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------------|
| < 5x | Cheap or distressed |
| 5-10x | Fair value |
| 10-15x | Moderate premium |
| 15-25x | Expensive |
| > 25x | Very expensive |

**Pitfalls:**
- EV/EBITDA varies wildly by industry (tech < 20x is normal, utilities > 10x is expensive)
- Always compare within sector
- Doesn't account for capital intensity (two companies with same EBITDA can have very different capital needs)

## 4. Profitability Metrics

### Gross Margin

**What it is:** The percentage of revenue left after subtracting the direct costs of making the product.

```
Gross Margin = (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue × 100%
```

**Example - Apple:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Revenue | $391 billion |
| COGS (Cost of Goods Sold) | $213 billion |
| Gross Profit | $178 billion |
| **Gross Margin** | **45.5%** |

**Why it matters:** Higher gross margin = more pricing power and/or more efficient production.

**Benchmark by industry:**

| Industry | Typical Gross Margin |
|----------|---------------------|
| Software (SaaS) | 70-90% |
| Consumer Brands | 40-60% |
| Retail | 20-30% |
| Airlines | 10-20% |
| Banks | N/A (different model) |

**What to look for:**
- Consistent or expanding margins over time
- Margins significantly above competitors = moat indicator
- Declining margins can signal competition eroding pricing power

---

### Operating Margin

**What it is:** The percentage of revenue left after ALL operating expenses (including R&D and SG&A).

```
Operating Margin = Operating Income / Revenue × 100%
```

**Example - Apple:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Revenue | $391 billion |
| COGS | $213 billion |
| Gross Profit | $178 billion |
| Operating Expenses (R&D + SG&A) | $58 billion |
| Operating Income | $120 billion |
| **Operating Margin** | **30.7%** |

**Why it matters:** Shows how efficiently the company runs its business.

---

### Net Margin

**What it is:** The percentage of revenue that becomes profit after EVERYTHING (taxes, interest, one-time items).

```
Net Margin = Net Income / Revenue × 100%
```

**Example - Apple:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Revenue | $391 billion |
| Operating Income | $120 billion |
| Interest & Taxes | $15 billion |
| Net Income | $105 billion |
| **Net Margin** | **26.9%** |

**How to interpret net margins:**

| Net Margin | Interpretation |
|------------|----------------|
| > 20% | Excellent — highly profitable business |
| 10-20% | Good — solid business |
| 5-10% | Average — competitive industry or heavy investment |
| < 5% | Low — thin margins, capital intensive, or troubled |
| Negative | Losing money |

**Pitfalls:**
- One-time items can distort net margin
- High net margin in one period might not be sustainable
- Compare within industry for meaningful analysis

---

### EBITDA Margin

**What it is:** EBITDA as a percentage of revenue. Useful for comparing companies with different capital structures and tax situations.

```
EBITDA Margin = EBITDA / Revenue × 100%
```

**Example - Comparing Airlines:**
| Company | EBITDA | Revenue | EBITDA Margin |
|---------|--------|---------|---------------|
| Delta | $9B | $54B | 16.7% |
| American Airlines | $7B | $53B | 13.2% |
| Southwest | $4B | $26B | 15.4% |

**Why it matters:** Removes the effects of debt (interest), taxes, and accounting decisions (depreciation) to compare pure operating profitability.

## 5. Growth Metrics

### Revenue Growth

**What it is:** The year-over-year percentage increase in revenue.

```
Revenue Growth = (Current Revenue - Prior Revenue) / Prior Revenue × 100%
```

**Example:**
| Year | Apple Revenue | YoY Growth |
|------|--------------|------------|
| 2022 | $394 billion | +7.4% |
| 2023 | $383 billion | -2.8% |
| 2024 | $391 billion | +2.1% |

**What "good" looks like:**

| Company Type | Strong Growth | Moderate | Slow/No Growth |
|--------------|--------------|----------|----------------|
| High Growth (Tech) | > 20% | 10-20% | < 10% |
| Medium Growth | > 10% | 5-10% | < 5% |
| Mature (Consumer) | > 5% | 2-5% | < 2% |
| Slow (Utilities) | > 3% | 1-3% | < 1% |

**Pitfalls:**
- Revenue growth without profit growth is not sustainable
- Acquisition-driven growth inflates revenue but often destroys value
- High past growth doesn't guarantee future growth

---

### EPS Growth

**What it is:** Year-over-year percentage increase in earnings per share.

```
EPS Growth = (Current EPS - Prior EPS) / Prior EPS × 100%
```

**Why it's better than revenue growth:** EPS accounts for buybacks (fewer shares = higher EPS) and shows actual profitability per shareholder.

**Example - Apple EPS:**
| Year | EPS | YoY Growth |
|------|-----|------------|
| 2022 | $6.11 | +8.9% |
| 2023 | $6.13 | +0.3% |
| 2024 | $6.77 | +10.4% |

---

### CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

**What it is:** The smoothed annual growth rate over a period of years. More accurate than simple average because it accounts for compounding.

```
CAGR = (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/years) - 1
```

**Example - Apple's revenue 2020 to 2024:**
| Year | Revenue |
|------|---------|
| 2020 | $274 billion |
| 2024 | $391 billion |

```
CAGR = ($391B / $274B)^(1/4) - 1 = (1.427)^0.25 - 1 = 0.093 = 9.3%
```

Apple's revenue grew at a 9.3% compound annual rate from 2020 to 2024.

**Why CAGR matters:** Simple averages can be misleading. If revenue grew 100% one year and fell 50% the next, the simple average is 25% — but you're back to where you started. CAGR would show 0%.

## 6. Quality and Return Metrics

### ROE (Return on Equity)

**What it is:** How much profit a company generates with the money shareholders have invested.

```
ROE = Net Income / Shareholder Equity × 100%
```

**Example - Apple:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Net Income (FY2024) | $105 billion |
| Average Shareholder Equity | $350 billion |
| **ROE** | **30%** |

**How to interpret ROE:**

| ROE | Interpretation |
|-----|----------------|
| > 25% | Excellent — generating strong returns |
| 15-25% | Good — solid business |
| 10-15% | Acceptable — meeting cost of capital |
| < 10% | Poor — may be destroying value |
| Negative | Losing money |

**Why ROE matters:** If a company has $100 million in equity and earns $30 million, that's a 30% ROE. If it earns $5 million, that's only 5% — you'd be better off in a savings account.

**Pitfalls:**
- ROE can be artificially inflated by:
  - **Debt** (less equity denominator = higher ROE)
  - **Share buybacks** (reduces equity)
  - **Accounting adjustments**
- Always check if ROE is accompanied by high debt

---

### ROIC (Return on Invested Capital)

**What it is:** How efficiently a company allocates ALL its capital (debt + equity) to generate returns. More complete than ROE.

```
ROIC = Net Operating Profit After Tax (NOPAT) / Invested Capital
```

**Example - Apple:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Operating Income | $120 billion |
| Tax Rate | ~15% |
| NOPAT | ~$102 billion |
| Invested Capital (Debt + Equity) | $600 billion |
| **ROIC** | **17%** |

**How to interpret ROIC:**

| ROIC | Interpretation |
|------|----------------|
| > 15% | Excellent — creates value |
| 10-15% | Good — generates reasonable returns |
| Cost of Capital (~8-10%) | Breaking even |
| < Cost of Capital | Destroying value |

**Why ROIC > ROE:** Warren Buffett calls ROIC "the single most important metric." It measures true capital efficiency regardless of how the company is financed.

**Pitfall:** ROIC vs WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital) is what matters. If ROIC > WACC, the company creates value. If ROIC < WACC, it destroys value even with positive net income.

## 7. Debt and Leverage Metrics

### Debt/Equity Ratio

**What it is:** The proportion of debt to equity financing. Shows how leveraged the company is.

```
Debt/Equity = Total Debt / Total Shareholder Equity
```

**Example:**
| Company | Total Debt | Equity | Ratio |
|---------|-----------|--------|-------|
| Apple | $110B | $350B | 0.31x |
| Verizon | $180B | $90B | 2.0x |
| Ford | $160B | $40B | 4.0x |

**How to interpret:**

| Ratio | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| < 0.5x | Conservative — low leverage |
| 0.5x - 1.5x | Moderate leverage |
| 1.5x - 3x | High leverage — cyclical industries OK |
| > 3x | Very high leverage — risky in downturns |

**Sector context matters:**

| Industry | Typical Debt/Equity |
|----------|-------------------|
| Utilities | 1.5x - 2.5x (stable cash flows support debt) |
| Banks | 5x - 10x (leveraged by nature) |
| Tech | 0x - 0.5x (don't need heavy debt) |
| Consumer Staples | 0.8x - 1.5x |
| Airlines | 2x - 5x (capital intensive) |

**Pitfalls:**
- Debt isn't automatically bad — utilities and banks NEED leverage
- High debt + recession = potential bankruptcy
- Always check if debt is growing faster than equity

---

### Debt/EBITDA

**What it is:** How many years of EBITDA it would take to pay off all debt. Measures debt sustainability.

```
Debt/EBITDA = Total Debt / EBITDA
```

**Example - Verizon:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Total Debt | $180 billion |
| EBITDA | $45 billion |
| **Debt/EBITDA** | **4.0x** |

**How to interpret:**

| Ratio | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| < 2x | Very healthy |
| 2x - 3x | Acceptable |
| 3x - 4x | Getting high — watch closely |
| 4x - 5x | High leverage |
| > 5x | Dangerous — may struggle to service debt |

**Pitfall:** Companies with Debt/EBITDA > 4-5x may struggle during economic downturns when EBITDA falls while debt stays constant.

---

### Current Ratio

**What it is:** Measures ability to pay short-term obligations with short-term assets.

```
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
```

**Example:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Current Assets | $150 billion |
| Current Liabilities | $140 billion |
| **Current Ratio** | **1.07x** |

**How to interpret:**

| Ratio | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| > 2.0 | Very healthy liquidity |
| 1.5 - 2.0 | Good |
| 1.0 - 1.5 | Acceptable |
| < 1.0 | Concerning — may struggle to pay bills |

**Pitfall:** Current ratio can be misleading for companies with very predictable cash flows (utilities can operate safely with lower ratios).

## 8. Cash Flow Metrics

### Free Cash Flow (FCF)

**What it is:** The cash a company generates after paying for its capital expenditures (investments in equipment, buildings, etc.). This is what the company can return to shareholders, pay down debt, or reinvest.

```
FCF = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures
```

**Why FCF > Net Income:** Net income includes non-cash items (depreciation) and can be manipulated through accounting choices. FCF is actual cash earned.

**Example - Apple FY2024:**
| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Operating Cash Flow | $118 billion |
| Capital Expenditures | $10 billion |
| **Free Cash Flow** | **$108 billion** |

**What to look for:**
- Consistent FCF generation over multiple years
- FCF should generally be positive and growing
- FCF should exceed or be close to net income (if FCF << Net Income, earnings may be inflated)

---

### FCF Yield

**What it is:** Free cash flow as a percentage of market cap. Shows how much cash return you're getting relative to the price.

```
FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow / Market Cap × 100%
```

**Example:**
| Company | Market Cap | FCF | FCF Yield |
|---------|-----------|-----|-----------|
| Apple | $2.9T | $108B | 3.7% |
| Bank of America | $340B | $30B | 8.8% |
| Amazon | $2.0T | $60B | 3.0% |

**How to interpret:**
- Higher FCF yield = potentially cheaper (more cash per dollar invested)
- FCF yield = inverse of EV/FCF multiple
- Compare to bond yields or other fixed income for context

---

## 9. Dividend Metrics

### Dividend Yield

**What it is:** The annual dividend as a percentage of the stock price. Shows your cash return on investment.

```
Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend Per Share / Stock Price × 100%
```

**Example:**
| Company | Stock Price | Annual Dividend | Dividend Yield |
|---------|-------------|----------------|----------------|
| Apple | $190 | $0.96 | 0.5% |
| Bank of America | $40 | $1.04 | 2.6% |
| AT&T | $18 | $1.11 | 6.2% |
| Verizon | $40 | $2.61 | 6.5% |

**How to interpret:**

| Yield | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| < 1% | Low yield — growth company or dividend may be at risk |
| 1-3% | Moderate — typical for quality companies |
| 3-5% | Good yield — higher than bonds |
| 5-8% | High yield — investigate WHY it's high |
| > 8% | Danger zone — likely a troubled company |

**The High Yield Trap:** A stock with 10% yield is almost always a TRAP. The market is pricing in a dividend cut. Always ask: "Is this dividend sustainable?"

---

### Payout Ratio

**What it is:** The percentage of earnings paid out as dividends.

```
Payout Ratio = Dividend Per Share / EPS × 100%
```

**Example:**
| Company | EPS | Dividend/Share | Payout Ratio |
|---------|-----|---------------|--------------|
| Apple | $6.77 | $0.96 | 14% |
| Bank of America | $3.11 | $1.04 | 33% |
| AT&T | $2.01 | $1.11 | 55% |
| Verizon | $4.92 | $2.61 | 53% |

**How to interpret:**

| Payout Ratio | Interpretation |
|--------------|----------------|
| < 30% | Very sustainable — lots of room to grow |
| 30-50% | Healthy — can maintain and grow dividends |
| 50-70% | Acceptable — little room for dividend cuts |
| 70-100% | Risky — vulnerable to earnings decline |
| > 100% | Unsustainable — paying more than earnings |

**Pitfalls:**
- Payout ratio > 100% means dividends are funded by debt or asset sales
- Energy companies often have high payout ratios due to cyclicality
- Focus on FCF/payout ratio instead of earnings-based when possible

---

### Dividend Growth Rate

**What it is:** The rate at which dividends increase over time.

**Example - Dividend Aristocrats (25+ years of increases):**
| Company | 5-Year Dividend CAGR |
|---------|---------------------|
| Apple | 10.2% |
| Johnson & Johnson | 6.0% |
| Coca-Cola | 3.5% |
| Procter & Gamble | 5.1% |

**Why dividend growth matters:**
- Dividends that grow faster than inflation preserve purchasing power
- Consistent dividend growth signals financial health
- Dividend growth investors compound returns over time

**What to look for:**
- Consistent 3-5 year track record of growing dividends
- Payout ratio remaining reasonable while growing dividends
- FCF sufficient to cover dividend many times over

## 10. Specialized Metrics

### Moats (Competitive Advantage)

**What it is:** Warren Buffett's term for a company's competitive advantage — what keeps competitors from stealing its customers and profits.

**Types of Moats:**

| Moat Type | What It Means | Example |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| **Brand** | Customers pay more for the brand | Apple, Coca-Cola, Rolex |
| **Switching Costs** | Too costly to switch to competitors | Microsoft Office, Banks |
| **Network Effects** | More users make product more valuable | Visa, Facebook, Airbnb |
| **Patents/IP** | Exclusive rights to technology | Pharmaceutical, Tech |
| **Regulatory** | Government protects from competition | Utilities, Gambling |
| **Cost Advantage** | Lower cost producer | Walmart, Amazon |

**How to identify a moat:**
- Gross margins consistently above competitors
- Pricing power (can raise prices without losing customers)
- Returns on capital (ROIC) consistently above cost of capital
- Market share stability or growth despite competition

**Warning signs of moat erosion:**
- Declining gross margins
- Increasing marketing spend to maintain sales
- Losing market share to lower-cost competitors
- Customers easily switching to alternatives

---

### Days to Cover

**What it is:** How many days it would take for all short sellers to cover their positions (at average trading volume).

```
Days to Cover = Short Interest / Average Daily Volume
```

**Example:**
| Company | Short Interest | Avg Daily Volume | Days to Cover |
|---------|---------------|------------------|---------------|
| GameStop | 50 million shares | 10 million shares/day | 5 days |
| Hertz | 100 million shares | 5 million shares/day | 20 days |

**How to interpret:**

| Days to Cover | Interpretation |
|---------------|----------------|
| < 3 days | Low short interest — normal |
| 3-5 days | Moderate |
| 5-10 days | High — vulnerable to squeeze |
| > 10 days | Very high — potential for short squeeze |

**The Short Squeeze Risk:** If bad news triggers buying, short sellers must buy shares to cover — creating a self-reinforcing rally. GameStop in 2021 was an extreme example.

---

### EV/EBIT

**What it is:** Like EV/EBITDA but includes depreciation and amortization.

```
EV/EBIT = Enterprise Value / EBIT
```

**EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes (Operating Income)**

**When to use EV/EBIT vs EV/EBITDA:**
- **EV/EBITDA** for capital-light businesses
- **EV/EBIT** for capital-intensive businesses (manufacturing, airlines, etc.)

**Example - Comparing two manufacturers:**
| Company | EV | EBITDA | D&A | EBIT | EV/EBITDA | EV/EBIT |
|---------|----|-------|-----|------|-----------|---------|
| Company A | $10B | $2B | $0.5B | $1.5B | 5.0x | 6.7x |
| Company B | $10B | $2B | $1.5B | $0.5B | 5.0x | 20x |

Company B is more capital intensive. EV/EBITDA makes it look similar to Company A, but EV/EBIT shows Company B is actually more expensive.

## 11. Putting It All Together

### The GARP Framework (Growth at Reasonable Price)

This strategy combines growth with value disciplines:

| Metric | Target | Why |
|--------|--------|-----|
| PEG Ratio | < 1.5 | Growth at reasonable price |
| ROE | > 15% | Quality company |
| ROIC | > 10% | Efficient capital use |
| Debt/Equity | < 1.0 | Conservative leverage |
| P/E | < 25x | Not overpaying |
| 5-Year EPS Growth | > 10% | Sustainable growth |
| FCF/Share | Growing | Cash generation |

### Red Flags Checklist

When screening or analyzing, watch for these warning signs:

| Metric | Red Flag |
|--------|----------|
| P/E | Very low but no clear reason |
| Revenue Growth | Much faster than competitors |
| Gross Margin | Declining despite "growth" |
| Net Income | Growing faster than revenue |
| Payout Ratio | > 100% |
| Dividend Yield | > 8% |
| Debt/EBITDA | > 5x |
| ROE | Very high (>30%) accompanied by very high debt |
| FCF | Consistently negative |
| Days to Cover | Rapidly increasing |

### The DuPont Analysis (Breaking Down ROE)

ROE can be decomposed to understand what's driving it:

```
ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage
ROE = (Net Income/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)
```

| Component | Measures | Higher Is |
|-----------|----------|----------|
| Net Margin | Profitability | Better |
| Asset Turnover | Efficiency | Better |
| Financial Leverage | Risk | Risky if too high |

**Example:**
| Company | Net Margin | Asset Turnover | Leverage | ROE |
|---------|------------|----------------|----------|-----|
| Company A | 15% | 0.8 | 1.5x | 18% |
| Company B | 10% | 1.2 | 2.0x | 24% |

Company B has lower margins but higher efficiency and leverage, resulting in higher ROE. But is that sustainable?
